DATA_ACT_AFTER_GO_LIVE

2026-03-28 7 MIN READ REGULATION

The EU Data Act stopped being a future planning item on 12 September 2025. If your team still treats portability and switching as procurement promises rather than product capabilities, you are already late.

WHY_THIS_CHANGED

The operational picture tightened across several EU instruments at once: the Data Act applies from 12 September 2025, DORA has applied since 17 January 2025, and NIS2 required Member States to adopt and publish national measures by 17 October 2024.

RESIDENCY_IS_NOT_THE_WHOLE_JOB

Teams often answer data-governance questions with a region selector and a storage diagram. That is not enough anymore. The real risk sits in exports, support access, backup restoration, analytics copies, logging, and the practical ability to leave a provider without a destructive rewrite.

When these pathways are undocumented, the business discovers too late that the data may be local but the operational dependency is not.

"If you cannot move the data cleanly, you do not really control the service."

WHAT_THE_DATA_ACT_CHANGES_IN_PRACTICE

The Data Act pushes portability and switching out of the nice-to-have column. For many teams that means building export jobs that work at production scale, defining handover boundaries between controller and processor roles, and proving that a service can be exited without losing business continuity.

The engineering consequence is simple: logs, backups, support tooling, and recovery workflows need to be treated as governed copies of the service, not as side systems that escape review because the main database sits in the right region.

WHERE_DORA_AND_NIS2_ENTER

DORA makes this operational for financial entities and their ICT supply chains by focusing on resilience, dependency oversight, and evidence. NIS2 does something similar for covered entities through national law, especially around risk-management measures and incident handling. The important point is not that the texts are identical. It is that the combined pressure removes the excuse for undocumented third-party dependence.

  • Keep a current map of where production data, backups, logs, and support access actually live.
  • Test export quality, not just export existence. Broken or partial exports are not an exit strategy.
  • Document provider dependencies, fallback routes, and the people who own cutover decisions.
  • Make incident evidence, restoration steps, and communication duties part of the same operating model.

Portability is no longer just a customer-rights talking point. It is now tangled up with resilience, supplier concentration, and how quickly an organization can recover under pressure.

WHAT_TO_FIX_NEXT

Most teams do not need a grand re-architecture first. They need a cleaner inventory and a more honest drill schedule. Start with the service boundaries that would matter during a provider change, a major outage, or a customer portability request.

  • Maintain a live map of data classes, region boundaries, subprocessors, and operational access paths.
  • Run export and migration exercises as part of recovery drills rather than treating them as procurement paperwork.
  • Define who owns evidence collection, incident notice inputs, and supplier coordination before a real event forces the answer.

WHO_SHOULD_READ_THIS

This article is for teams that build or operate data-heavy services and need to connect legal dates with implementation work. It is not legal advice. It is a planning brief for the people who own product architecture, platform operations, procurement evidence, and provider exit readiness.
